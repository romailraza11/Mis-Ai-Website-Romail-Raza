Malware and Cyber Threats

The WannaCry Ransomware Attack

The WannaCry ransomware attack, which began on May 12, 2017, is one of the clearest examples of how dangerous malware can be when cybersecurity practices are weak. The attack exploited a known vulnerability in Windows systems and encrypted files on affected devices, forcing organizations to either pay a ransom or lose access to essential data. The impact was severe because hospitals, businesses, and public services depended on their systems to function properly. This case showed that cybersecurity is not just a technical issue; it is also a matter of public trust, operational continuity, and preparedness.

I was especially interested in how quickly the attack spread and how much damage it caused to organizations that had not updated their systems. It reminded me that even a single overlooked weakness can create major consequences. In the modern digital world, security is not limited to protecting personal devices; it also includes safeguarding networks, public infrastructure, and sensitive information that affects large groups of people. The lesson from WannaCry is that prevention matters just as much as recovery, and that a strong security strategy requires regular updates, backups, monitoring, and education.

Types of Malware

  1. Ransomware: locks or encrypts files and demands payment to restore access.
  2. Trojan: disguises itself as legitimate software to trick users into downloading it.
  3. Spyware: secretly monitors activity and collects private information without consent.
  4. Virus: spreads by attaching itself to files or programs and infecting other devices.

Common Hacker Motives

  1. Financial gain through extortion, theft, fraud, or data sales.
  2. Political or ideological motives, such as disruption or public attention.
  3. Espionage to gather information for strategic or competitive advantage.
  4. Reputation or challenge, where attackers attempt to demonstrate technical skill or power.

Prevention Strategies

  • Use multi-factor authentication and unique passwords for every important account.
  • Install software and operating system updates promptly to reduce vulnerabilities.
  • Create regular backups and store them in secure locations that are not always connected to your main network.
  • Train users to identify phishing emails, suspicious links, and unfamiliar downloads.
  • Use trusted security software and restrict access to data based on necessity.
